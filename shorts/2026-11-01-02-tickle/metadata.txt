TITLE: Why Can't You Tickle Yourself? #shorts
DESCRIPTION:
Why can't you tickle yourself? Someone else's finger can make you squirm, but your own finger on the same spot feels dull. The leading explanation: every time you move, your brain sends a copy of the command, a forecast of what you'll feel, and turns that expected feeling down. In 1998, brain scans found the brain's touch area reacted less to self-made touch. In 1999, people moved a robot arm that stroked their own palm: adding a delay of just 0.2 s, or twisting the direction, made the same touch feel more ticklish. So a tickle is really about surprise. What else does your brain quietly cancel?

How the unseen works: sensory prediction and the attenuation of self-produced touch.

Sources:
- Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith (1998), Central cancellation of self-produced tickle sensation, Nature Neuroscience 1:635-640: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10196573/
- Blakemore, Frith & Wolpert (1999), Spatio-temporal prediction modulates the perception of self-produced stimuli, Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience 11:551-559: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10511643/

Notes: self-touch is weakened, not switched off, and people differ. The 1998 fMRI result is a measured difference in brain activity; the "forecast" (internal prediction) is the authors' proposed explanation. In the 1999 study, people moved one robot with their left hand and a second robot touched their right palm; delays of 0 to 200 ms and rotations of 0 to 90 degrees raised tickliness ratings (small lab samples). Robot-arm footage and all diagrams are illustrations, not the study's equipment.
