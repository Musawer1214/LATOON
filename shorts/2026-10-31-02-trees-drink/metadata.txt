TITLE: How Does a Tree Drink Without a Pump? #shorts
DESCRIPTION:
How does a tree drink without a pump? An ordinary suction pump can only lift water about ten metres, yet redwoods grow past 110 m. The answer: evaporation at the leaves pulls unbroken threads of water up through the xylem, so the water inside a tree is under negative pressure, literally stretched. What else around you runs on stretched water?

How the unseen works: the cohesion-tension mechanism of sap ascent, measured as negative xylem pressure and reproduced in a gel "synthetic tree".

Sources:
- Scholander, Bradstreet, Hemmingsen & Hammel (1965), Sap Pressure in Vascular Plants, Science 148:339: https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.148.3668.339
- Wheeler & Stroock (2008), The transpiration of water at negative pressures in a synthetic tree, Nature 455:208: https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07226
- Dixon & Joly (1895), On the ascent of sap, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 186:563: https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.1895.0012
- Koch, Sillett, Jennings & Davis (2004), The limits to tree height, Nature 428:851 (tallest known tree 112.7 m): https://www.nature.com/articles/nature02417
- Hayward (1970), Mechanical pump with a suction lift of 17 metres, Nature 225:376 (atmosphere supports ~10.4 m of water): https://www.nature.com/articles/225376b0
- Choat et al. (2018), Triggers of tree mortality under drought, Nature 558:531: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0240-x

Notes: "about ten metres" is the atmospheric limit for an ordinary suction pump; Hayward (1970) built a special pump that beat it using water under tension. The -5 / -80 atmosphere figures are from Scholander's 1965 abstract ("-4 or -5 atmospheres in a damp forest to -80 atmospheres in the desert").
