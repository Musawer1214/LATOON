## 0:32 — A desperate remedy: Pauli to 1956

The story starts in 1930. Wolfgang Pauli saw energy going missing when some atoms decay. As a desperate remedy, he proposed a tiny neutral particle that carries it away, unseen. Why so hard? A neutrino has no electric charge and almost no mass, so it almost never touches the matter it flies through. With a detector the size of a person, you would wait about a hundred years to see one neutrino hit.

So the trick is not a cleverer trap. It is a bigger one. Think of shooting stars. Watch one small patch of sky, and you may wait all night. Watch the whole sky, and you will see several.

**[1:26] P06** (read 21.3 s, slot 23.3 s)  
Physicists wanted the rarest neutrinos of all: the most energetic ones, from deep space. Space also throws charged particles at us, called cosmic rays. But magnetic fields bend their paths, so we cannot tell where they came from. Neutrinos are made in the same violent places, and nothing bends them. They point straight back home.

In the 1980s, Francis Halzen, a physicist at the University of Wisconsin, had a bold idea. Why build a giant tank of water, when Antarctica already has one, frozen solid? Deep ice is completely dark. No life, very little radioactivity, no earthquakes.

**[3:12] P12** (read 14.8 s, slot 16.4 s)  
But if neutrinos pass through everything, what does the ice see? Very rarely, a neutrino smashes into an atom's nucleus. The crash creates a new charged particle, often a muon, flying on in nearly the same direction.
