DESCRIPTION:
How was Enigma broken? Germany's cipher machine had about 159 quintillion starting settings, yet it carried one hidden flaw: no letter could ever be encrypted as itself. This is the story of how that tiny rule, plus a few human habits, opened the most trusted secret machine of World War II.

We follow it from the inside out: how Enigma's rotors, plugboard and reflector scrambled every keypress; why the reflector made "A is never A" unavoidable; how Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski of Poland's Cipher Bureau first broke it in 1932 and handed their methods to Britain and France in July 1939; how Bletchley Park used cribs like "keine besonderen Ereignisse" (nothing to report); how Alan Turing's Bombe, improved by Gordon Welchman's diagonal board, hunted for settings without contradictions; and how Mavis Lever spotted a message with not a single L. We finish with Kerckhoffs's principle and what modern ciphers like AES learned from it.
