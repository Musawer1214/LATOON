## The Question He Refused (1950)  (~0:55)

**06** · `C1-01` · ~0:55 — Manchester, 1950. Alan Turing, by then programming the Manchester computer, publishes a paper in the journal Mind. Its first line: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" [T1] [T9]

**07** · `C1-02` · ~1:07 — Then he refuses to define the words. If "think" means whatever people commonly mean by it, he wrote, the answer would have to come from something like a Gallup poll. "But this is absurd." [T2]

**08** · `C1-03` · ~1:20 — He swaps the question for a game. An interrogator types questions to two hidden players and must tell which is which. First a man and a woman; then Turing asks, what if a machine takes one seat? [T4]

**09** · `C1-04` · ~1:35 — He called it the imitation game; we call it the Turing test. And he bet that in about fifty years, an average interrogator would have no more than a seventy percent chance of spotting the machine after five minutes. [T3]

**10** · `C1-05` · ~1:51 — Notice: he didn't bet that machines would think. He bet that by the end of the century, people would talk of machines thinking "without expecting to be contradicted." A prediction about machines, and about us. [T3]

**11** · `C1-06` · ~2:05 — He even noted that we already run this test on each other: the viva voce, the spoken exam that finds out whether someone really understands, or has "learnt it parrot fashion." [T5]

**12** · `C1-07` · ~2:17 — If you've sat a viva in Peshawar or Lahore, you know the move: the examiner digs until it's clear which one you are. We even have a word for learning parrot fashion: ratta. Keep that word in mind. [L1]

**13** · `C1-08` · ~2:33 — But first, Turing had to answer an objection written a hundred and seven years earlier. [T6] [T8]


## The Countess's Objection (1843)  (~2:40)

**14** · `C2-01` · ~2:40 — London, 1843. Ada Lovelace publishes long notes on Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, a steam-powered computer he designed but never completed. [T6] [T7]

**15** · `C2-02` · ~2:48 — Her notes are famous for showing how such a machine could be programmed. And in them she warned: "The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform." [T6] [T7]

**16** · `C2-03` · ~3:04 — It is the oldest objection in this debate, and it still sounds right: a machine only does what it is told.

**17** · `C2-04` · ~3:13 — Turing's first answer is disarmingly honest. "Machines take me by surprise with great frequency," he wrote, mostly because he never calculated carefully enough to know what they would do. [T6]

**18** · `C2-05` · ~3:24 — His deeper answer was a different machine: not a programmed adult mind but something like a child's, "a notebook as one buys it from the stationer's": little mechanism, lots of blank sheets. Then teach it. [T10]

**19** · `C2-06` · ~3:38 — A machine that learns, instead of only following orders. For decades, that stayed an idea. Then machines started beating us at our own games. [T10]


## Move 37 (1997-2016)  (~3:49)

**20** · `C3-01` · ~3:49 — New York, 1997. IBM's Deep Blue beats world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match, by force: two hundred million positions every second, steered by evaluations its team built in. Lovelace would not have been surprised. [G1]

**21** · `C3-02` · ~4:04 — Seoul, March 2016. DeepMind's AlphaGo plays Lee Sedol, one of the greatest Go players of his era. AlphaGo was not handed a strategy. It learned from thousands of expert games, then by playing against itself. [G2]

**22** · `C3-03` · ~4:18 — In game two it played move 37, a move DeepMind puts at a one in ten thousand chance of being played. It helped win the game. AlphaGo took the match, four games to one. [G2]

**23** · `C3-04` · ~4:32 — Lee Sedol said he had thought AlphaGo was "merely a machine." "But when I saw this move, I changed my mind. Surely, AlphaGo is creative." [G2]

**24** · `C3-05` · ~4:42 — So here was a machine that did something nobody had ordered, and surprised a master. Lovelace's objection was cracking. But a sharper one was waiting: surprise is not understanding. In 1980, a philosopher built a room to prove it. [S1]


## The Room (1980)  (~4:59)

**25** · `C4-01` · ~4:59 — John Searle, at Berkeley, asks you to imagine him locked in a room. He does not read a word of Chinese. To him, in his words, it is "just so many meaningless squiggles." [S1]

**26** · `C4-02` · ~5:12 — Slips of Chinese writing come in under the door. He has a rulebook in English: when you see these shapes, send back those shapes. He follows it perfectly, and to the people outside, his answers are indistinguishable from a native speaker's. [S2]

**27** · `C4-03` · ~5:28 — Yet inside the room, nothing understands Chinese. Searle's conclusion: running the right program is not enough for understanding. In his words, a program has "only a syntax but no semantics." [S2]

**28** · `C4-04` · ~5:40 — Remember ratta? The Chinese Room is the perfect ratta: every answer right, and none of it understood. [L1]

**29** · `C4-05` · ~5:48 — The most famous comeback is the systems reply. The man doesn't understand, but he is only one part. The whole system, man plus rulebook plus paper, understands. [S3]

**30** · `C4-06` · ~5:59 — Searle's answer: fine. Let him memorise the rulebook and do everything in his head. Now he is the whole system. And he still understands nothing. [S3]

**31** · `C4-07` · ~6:09 — So who understands Chinese here: the man, the book, or nobody at all? Philosophers have argued about it for more than forty years. [S1]

**32** · `C4-08` · ~6:19 — And one twist people forget. Searle never said machines can't think. "We are precisely such machines," he wrote. His claim was that the brain's biology has the causal powers that matter, and a program alone does not. A simulated rainstorm, he pointed out, leaves nobody drenched. [S4] [S5]

**33** · `C4-09` · ~6:37 — Simulate a monsoon over Lahore all day long; Lahore stays dry. [L2]


## A Board Inside the Machine (2022-2023)  (~6:43)

**34** · `C5-01` · ~6:43 — Searle's rulebook was something a person could read. Today's language models have no rulebook anyone wrote, only billions of learned numbers, tuned to predict the next piece of text. [M1]

**35** · `C5-02` · ~6:55 — So does anything like understanding grow inside? In 2022, researchers at Harvard, MIT and Northeastern tested it in a tiny world. They trained a GPT-style model on move lists from the board game Othello, twenty million games. It never saw a board. Nobody told it the rules. [M2]

**36** · `C5-03` · ~7:13 — It learned to propose legal moves almost perfectly. Trained on the big set, its top choice was illegal about once in ten thousand. [M2]

**37** · `C5-04` · ~7:23 — Then they looked inside. With small probes, they could read the board out of the model's internal activity, square by square: a picture of a game it had never been shown. [M2]

**38** · `C5-05` · ~7:35 — Then the decisive step. They edited that inner picture, flipping a disc inside the model, and its next moves changed to fit the edited board. The map was not decoration. The model was using it. [M2]

**39** · `C5-06` · ~7:50 — That is real evidence of an inner model of a world. But it is a toy world. And big language models can also fail in surprising ways. [M2]

**40** · `C5-07` · ~8:01 — In 2023, researchers asked GPT-4 questions like "Who is Tom Cruise's mother?" It got those right seventy-nine percent of the time. Flip them around, "Who is Mary Lee Pfeiffer's son?", and it dropped to thirty-three. Same fact, other direction: the reversal curse. [M4]

**41** · `C5-08` · ~8:17 — So: a map of the world inside, or a very good parrot? So far, some of both, depending on what you test. If a machine plays by a map in its head, does it understand the game? Tell us where you'd draw the line. [M2] [M4]


## The Test Is Passed (2025-2026)  (~8:36)

**42** · `C6-01` · ~8:36 — Back to that seventy-three percent. The study, by Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen, was peer-reviewed and published in PNAS in 2026. [F1]

**43** · `C6-02` · ~8:45 — It used Turing's three-way setup: each interrogator chatted with a human and a machine at once, for five minutes, then picked the human. Two separate groups took part: university students, and paid volunteers online. [F1]

**44** · `C6-03` · ~8:58 — With the persona, GPT-4.5 was picked as the human seventy-three percent of the time, and LLaMa 3.1 fifty-six. Without it, the same models fell to thirty-six and thirty-eight. ELIZA, a 1960s rule-based chatbot added as a check, got twenty-three. [F2] [F3]

**45** · `C6-04` · ~9:14 — When they stretched the games to fifteen minutes, it held. GPT-5, with the same persona, was picked fifty-nine percent of the time. [F4]

**46** · `C6-05` · ~9:23 — So Turing's bet came true, about twenty-five years late. His line was seventy percent accuracy for the interrogator. Here, people facing the best model did worse than a coin toss. [T3] [F2]

**47** · `C6-06` · ~9:35 — But look at how people decided. The most common strategy was small talk; only twelve percent tested knowledge or reasoning, the things Turing imagined. And the reason given most often was linguistic style: a typo, a casual tone, the feel of the writing. [F5]

**48** · `C6-07` · ~9:52 — Ghalib has a line that fits this almost too well: "They say Ghalib's way of saying things is something else." He meant his poetry. The judges did the same thing: they chose by the manner of speaking. [Q1]

**49** · `C6-08` · ~10:07 — The authors' careful conclusion: the test may now measure humanlikeness more than intelligence. It shows a machine can stand in for a person in a short chat; not what, if anything, is going on inside. [F6]


## Thinking Is Not Feeling  (~10:22)

**50** · `C7-01` · ~10:22 — And inside is where the hardest question lives. Thinking is one thing. Experiencing, there being something it feels like to be you, is another. Turing saw that objection coming too. [T11]

**51** · `C7-02` · ~10:34 — In 1949, Professor Jefferson argued that no machine equals a brain until it writes a sonnet because of thoughts and emotions felt. Turing's reply: taken to the extreme, the only way to be sure anything thinks is to be it, and that would apply to other people too. So, he wrote, we keep "the polite convention that everyone thinks." [T11]

**52** · `C7-03` · ~10:58 — In 2023, nineteen researchers, including the AI pioneer Yoshua Bengio, tried to do better than a polite convention. [B1]

**53** · `C7-04` · ~11:06 — They took leading scientific theories of consciousness, such as global workspace theory, and turned them into "indicator properties": concrete features you could check for in a real AI system. [B1]

**54** · `C7-05` · ~11:17 — Their 2023 analysis suggested no current AI systems were conscious, and also found no obvious technical barriers to building systems that meet the indicators. Their warning: meeting them still would not prove a system is conscious. [B2]

**55** · `C7-06` · ~11:32 — Even that method rests on a bet, a mainstream but disputed one: that consciousness comes from the right computations, whatever the machine is made of. Supporters of integrated information theory argue a digital computer is unlikely to be conscious, whatever program it runs. [B3]

**56** · `C7-07` · ~11:49 — Notice the shape of that fight. Is it the pattern that matters, or the stuff it runs on? It is Turing against Searle all over again, now argued over real machines. [B3] [S4]
