## The Question He Refused (1950)  (~1:16)

**06** · `C1-01` · ~1:16 — It's 1950. Alan Turing, who was by then programming one of the world's first computers, publishes a paper in a journal called Mind. And in its very first line, he puts the question: can machines think? [T1] [T9]

**07** · `C1-02` · ~1:31 — But right after that, he refuses to get tangled up in defining the words. If 'thinking' simply means whatever ordinary people take it to mean, he said, then to find the answer we would have to run something like a public survey. And that, Turing wrote, would be absurd. [T2]

**08** · `C1-03` · ~1:50 — So he replaces the question with a game. One person types questions to two players they cannot see, and has to work out who is who. At first, the two players are a man and a woman. Then Turing asks: what if we put a machine in place of one of them? [T4]

**09** · `C1-04` · ~2:11 — Turing called it the Imitation Game. Today we call it the Turing Test. He predicted that in about fifty years, after a five-minute conversation, an average person would have no more than a 70 percent chance of spotting the machine. [T3]

**10** · `C1-05` · ~2:27 — Listen carefully to this part. Turing did not bet that machines would really start thinking. He said that by the end of the century, people would talk about machines thinking in a normal way, and nobody would really object. So the prediction was not only about machines. It was about us, too. [T3]

**11** · `C1-06` · ~2:47 — Turing wrote something else interesting. We already run a test like this on each other: the viva voce, the spoken exam. The teacher asks question after question to find out whether the student has really understood, or has simply memorised it like a parrot. [T5]

**12** · `C1-07` · ~3:05 — If you've ever sat a viva at an exam centre, you'll know the scene. The examiner twists the question a little, then digs deeper, until it's clear whether you understand it or have only memorised it. We even have our own word for that: ratta. Keep that word in mind. [L1]

**13** · `C1-08` · ~3:25 — But before all this, Turing had to answer an objection that had been raised 107 years earlier. [T6] [T8]


## The Countess's Objection (1843)  (~3:33)

**14** · `C2-01` · ~3:33 — The year is 1843. Ada Lovelace publishes detailed notes on Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine. It was a design for a steam-powered computer, which Babbage worked on but was never able to finish. [T6] [T7]

**15** · `C2-02` · ~3:46 — These notes became famous because they explained how such a machine could be programmed. But Ada also made one important point: the Analytical Engine does not claim to create anything new on its own. It can only do what we know how to order it to do. [T6] [T7]

**16** · `C2-03` · ~4:04 — This is probably the oldest objection in the whole debate about machines. And even today, it sounds right: in the end, a machine only does what it is told to do.

**17** · `C2-04` · ~4:17 — Turing's first answer was very honest. He wrote that machines surprise him quite often. But he also admitted that this mostly happens because he hadn't worked out in advance exactly what the machine would do. [T6]

**18** · `C2-05` · ~4:31 — His real answer was a different kind of machine. Not one filled in advance with a whole adult mind, but one that could learn like a child. Turing gave an example: a new notebook bought from a stationery shop, with a little bit of structure at the start and lots of blank pages. Then you teach it. [T10]

**19** · `C2-06` · ~4:53 — In other words, a machine that doesn't just follow orders, but also learns. For decades, that remained only an idea. Then machines started beating us at our own games. [T10]


## Move 37 (1997-2016)  (~5:06)

**20** · `C3-01` · ~5:06 — In 1997, IBM's chess computer, Deep Blue, beat world champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match. Its strength was speed: it checked 200 million positions every second, using rules for judging those positions that its team had set in advance. Ada Lovelace probably wouldn't have been surprised by that result. [G1]

**21** · `C3-02` · ~5:26 — In March 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo faced Lee Sedol, one of the greatest Go players of his time. Nobody handed AlphaGo a ready-made strategy. It first learned from thousands of games played by experts, and then kept learning by playing against itself. [G2]

**22** · `C3-03` · ~5:42 — In the second game, AlphaGo played Move 37: a move that, by DeepMind's estimate, had only a one in ten thousand chance of being played. That move helped it win the game. And in the end, AlphaGo won the match four games to one. [G2]

**23** · `C3-04` · ~6:00 — Lee Sedol said afterwards: I thought AlphaGo was just a machine. But when I saw that move, I changed my mind. I felt AlphaGo was truly creative. [G2]

**24** · `C3-05` · ~6:11 — So now we had a machine that made a move nobody had specifically told it to make, and surprised a great master. Lovelace's objection began to look weaker. But there was another, tougher objection waiting: surprising someone is not the same as understanding. In 1980, a philosopher used the example of a room to explain this. [S1]


## The Room (1980)  (~6:34)

**25** · `C4-01` · ~6:34 — The philosopher John Searle says: imagine I'm locked inside a room. I don't know a single word of Chinese. To me, anything written in Chinese is just meaningless squiggles. [S1]

**26** · `C4-02` · ~6:46 — Notes written in Chinese are slipped in under the door. I have a rulebook in English. It says: when you see these symbols, send back those symbols. I follow it exactly. And to the Chinese speakers outside, my replies look as if a native Chinese speaker wrote them. [S2]

**27** · `C4-03` · ~7:05 — But inside the room, I don't understand any of the Chinese at all. That was Searle's point: just running the right program does not create understanding. In his words, a program has syntax, the rules for the symbols, but no semantics, their meaning. [S2]

**28** · `C4-04` · ~7:22 — Remember ratta? The Chinese Room is the perfect example of it. Not a single answer is wrong, yet the one giving the answers has no idea what any of them mean. [L1]

**29** · `C4-05` · ~7:35 — The most famous reply to this is called the Systems Reply. It says: fine, the man sitting in the room doesn't understand. But he isn't alone. The man, the rulebook and the paper together make one whole system. Their claim was that the understanding lies in that whole system. [S3]

**30** · `C4-06` · ~7:55 — Searle replied: all right, let's accept that. Now let the man memorise the whole rulebook and do every step in his own head. Now he himself is the whole system. And still, he doesn't understand any Chinese. [S3]

**31** · `C4-07` · ~8:09 — So who, in this room, actually understands Chinese? The man? The book? Or nobody at all? Philosophers have been arguing about exactly this for more than forty years. [S1]

**32** · `C4-08` · ~8:21 — And here is something people often miss. Searle never said that machines cannot think. He even wrote that we humans are ourselves a kind of machine. His objection was that the brain's biology has a special power that does not come just from running a program, just as a computer simulation of rain doesn't make anyone wet. [S4] [S5]

**33** · `C4-09` · ~8:44 — You could run a simulation of a fire on your computer all day, and the room wouldn't get even slightly warm. [L2]


## A Board Inside the Machine (2022-2023)  (~8:53)

**34** · `C5-01` · ~8:53 — Searle's rulebook was something a person could read. But inside today's language models, there is no book that someone sat down and wrote. Instead, they contain billions of learned numbers, tuned so they can guess what the next piece of text will be. [M1]

**35** · `C5-02` · ~9:11 — So does something like real understanding form inside them? In 2022, a team of researchers tried to test this in a small, simple world. They trained a GPT-style model on the moves from twenty million games of a board game called Othello. The model never saw a board, and no one ever told it the rules. [M2]

**36** · `C5-03` · ~9:33 — Even so, it learned to suggest a legal move almost every time. After training on the large dataset, its top choice broke the rules only about once in ten thousand moves. [M2]

**37** · `C5-04` · ~9:45 — Then the researchers looked inside the model. Using small probes, they could read the state of the board from its internal activity, square by square. In other words, the model held a map of a board it had never been shown. [M2]

**38** · `C5-05` · ~10:02 — Then they took one more step. In that inner map, they flipped a single disc. And the model's next moves changed to match the edited board. So the map wasn't just sitting there. The model was actually using it to make its decisions. [M2]

**39** · `C5-06` · ~10:19 — That is solid evidence that a model can build a map of a world inside itself. But it was a small world, just a board game. And large language models can still make mistakes in many strange ways. [M2]

**40** · `C5-07` · ~10:34 — For example, in 2023, researchers asked GPT-4: who is Tom Cruise's mother? It answered questions like that correctly 79 percent of the time. Then they turned the question around: who is Mary Lee Pfeiffer's son? Now only 33 percent of the answers were correct. The fact was the same; only the direction of the question had changed. This is called the Reversal Curse. [M4]

**41** · `C5-08` · ~10:59 — So is there a map of the world inside, or just a very good parrot that has learned everything by rote? So far, the answer is that there is some evidence on both sides, depending on what you test. If a machine is playing with the help of a map it built inside itself, does it actually understand the game? Where would you draw the line? Tell us in the comments. [M2] [M4]


## The Test Is Passed (2025-2026)  (~11:28)

**42** · `C6-01` · ~11:28 — Now let's come back to that 73 percent. The study was carried out by Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen. After peer review, it was published in 2026 in the journal PNAS. [F1]

**43** · `C6-02` · ~11:41 — It used Turing's own method. Each questioner chatted with a human and a machine at the same time for five minutes, and then said which one was the human. Two separate groups took part: university students, and paid volunteers who joined online. [F1]

**44** · `C6-03` · ~11:57 — When a model was given a specific persona, a character to speak as, GPT-4.5 was taken for the human 73 percent of the time, and LLaMA 3.1, 56 percent. But without that character, those figures fell to 36 and 38 percent. ELIZA, a simple chatbot from the 1960s, was also included for comparison. It was picked as the human only 23 percent of the time. [F2] [F3]

**45** · `C6-04` · ~12:23 — Then the conversations were made longer, fifteen minutes each. The result still held. GPT-5, with the same persona, was taken for the human 59 percent of the time. [F4]

**46** · `C6-05` · ~12:34 — So Turing's prediction came true, about twenty-five years late. He had said the questioner would not identify the machine correctly more than 70 percent of the time. Here, against the best model, people did even worse than tossing a coin. [T3] [F2]

**47** · `C6-06` · ~12:50 — But the really interesting part is how people made their decision. Most of them just made small talk. Only 12 percent tested knowledge or reasoning, the way Turing had imagined. And the most common reason for their choice was the style of writing: a typing mistake, a casual tone, or simply the feeling that a human was writing on the other side. [F5]

**48** · `C6-07` · ~13:15 — This brings to mind a line from the poet Ghalib: people say that Ghalib's way of expressing things is something else. Ghalib was talking about his poetry. But here, people did much the same thing. Instead of looking at what was inside the answers, they judged by the way things were said. [Q1]

**49** · `C6-08` · ~13:36 — That's why the study's authors drew their conclusion very carefully. According to them, this test may now measure how human a machine seems more than how intelligent it is. It shows that a machine can take a person's place in a short chat. It doesn't tell us what is happening inside, or whether anything is happening at all. [F6]


## Thinking Is Not Feeling  (~14:00)

**50** · `C7-01` · ~14:00 — And this brings us to the hardest question of all. Thinking is one thing. But feeling something, having the inner experience of being someone, is a completely different thing. Turing knew that this objection would come up too. [T11]

**51** · `C7-02` · ~14:15 — In 1949, Professor Jefferson argued that no machine could equal a brain until it could write a sonnet, a special kind of poem, out of thoughts and emotions it had really felt. Turing replied: if we set that condition, then the only way to be completely sure that anyone thinks would be to become that person ourselves. And that problem would apply to other humans too. So in everyday life, we simply assume that other people think as well. [T11]

**52** · `C7-03` · ~14:47 — In 2023, nineteen researchers tried to go one step further than that. Among them was Yoshua Bengio, one of the biggest names in AI. [B1]

**53** · `C7-04` · ~14:56 — They took the leading scientific theories of consciousness, such as Global Workspace Theory, and from them drew a set of clear signs that could be checked in a real AI system. These were called indicator properties. [B1]

**54** · `C7-05` · ~15:11 — According to their 2023 analysis, no AI system at that time qualified as conscious by these standards. But they also saw no clear technical barrier to building a system that meets these signs. Their warning, though, was this: even if all the signs are met, that still would not prove that consciousness is there. [B2]

**55** · `C7-06` · ~15:32 — And behind this method there is also a big question. Can consciousness come only from the right kind of computation, no matter what the machine is made of? Many experts believe so, but not everyone agrees. Some supporters of Integrated Information Theory say an ordinary digital computer may never be conscious, whatever program it runs. [B3]

**56** · `C7-07` · ~15:54 — If you think about it, the debate has come right back to where it started. Is the real thing the pattern running inside, or the material it runs on? In a way, it's Turing and Searle face to face once again. Only now, the argument isn't about imaginary machines. It's about real ones. [B3] [S4]
